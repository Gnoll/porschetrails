Sixty-two years ago this month, Porsche took its new car to the Paris Motor Show. It was called the 901. Within weeks it had a different name, and that name has been on the back of Porsche's best-known car ever since.

## A successor to the 356

Porsche first showed the car at the Frankfurt Motor Show in September 1963, as the successor to the [356](/models/356/). It was larger and more practical than the car it replaced, with a body designed under Ferdinand Alexander "Butzi" Porsche and a new 2.0-litre air-cooled flat-six of 130 PS, mounted behind the rear axle.

Development took another year. Series production began in September 1964, and the next month Porsche exhibited the car at the Paris Motor Show, still badged as the 901.

## Peugeot's objection

Paris was where the trouble started. Peugeot had patented three-digit model names with a zero in the middle, and claimed the rights to that pattern in France. It objected to Porsche selling a car called 901 in its home market.

Rather than sell the car under a different name in one market, Porsche chose a simple fix: keep the number and change the middle digit. The 0 became a 1, and the 901 became the 911.

## The first 82 cars

The change came early enough that very few 901s exist. Porsche says 82 cars were built under the original name, all between 14 September and 16 November 1964. The factory kept them for testing and exhibition rather than selling them to private customers, although some later passed into private hands. Customer sales of the 911 took several more months.

Surviving 901s are rare. The car in the photograph above is one of them, shown unrestored.

## Why it still matters

The name has outlasted every part of the original car. Engines have grown from 2.0 litres to well over 3.0, gained water cooling, turbochargers and now hybrid assistance, and the body has been redrawn several times. The three digits on the engine lid have stayed the same.
