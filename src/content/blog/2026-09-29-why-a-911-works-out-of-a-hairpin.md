Putting the engine behind the rear axle is, on paper, an odd thing to do. Porsche has spent sixty years turning it into an advantage, and the place you feel that advantage most is the exit of a hairpin.

## What is happening

A 911 carries a little over 60 per cent of its weight on the rear wheels when standing still. When you accelerate, weight transfers rearward in any car. In a 911 it is added to a rear axle that is already heavily loaded, so the driven tyres are pressed into the road just when they are asked to do the most work.

A front-engined, rear-drive car has less weight over its driven wheels to begin with. A front-drive car is worse off again, because acceleration takes load away from the wheels that are pulling it. That is why a rear-drive 911 can use more of its power, sooner, out of a slow corner than cars with considerably more of it.

## The other side of the bargain

The same weight has to be managed on the way in. With the mass at the back, the nose is light, so the front tyres need to be loaded by braking before they will turn the car. And if you lift off sharply in the middle of a corner, weight comes off the rear and the heavy tail can start to swing.

Decades of development have tamed this. Multi-link rear suspension arrived with the [993](/models/911-993/), wider rear tyres and stability control followed, and rear-wheel steering on recent cars helps further. The physics have not changed, though, and the classic advice still holds:

- **Brake in a straight line**, and finish most of it before you turn.
- **Turn in on a trailing brake or a neutral throttle**, so the nose has some weight on it.
- **Be patient**, then open the throttle progressively once the car is pointing at the exit.

Slow in, fast out. On a road like the [Stelvio](/trails/stelvio-pass/) or the [Great Dolomites Road](/trails/great-dolomites-road/), where each hairpin is followed by a short uphill straight, that is also the smooth and safe way to drive.

## What about the others?

- **Carrera 4 and Turbo.** Four-wheel drive sends some torque forward when the rears begin to slip. It adds security in the wet more than outright traction in the dry.
- **Cayman and Boxster.** The engine sits ahead of the rear axle, giving a more even balance, a lighter feel on turn-in and less of the pendulum effect. Traction is very good, though not quite at 911 level.
- **Taycan.** With a motor on each axle and a heavy battery low in the floor, traction is managed electronically and is immense. The weight is what you notice on the way in.

## On a wet road

The traction advantage is still there in the rain, but so is the tail. Leave the stability systems on, use Wet mode if your car has it, and treat painted lines and polished hairpin apexes with respect.
